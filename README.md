# The Solar Paradox: Industrial Abundance and the Limits of China's Electricity System

Declan Carroll · 26 September 2026

![Illustrative solar array and transmission corridor at dusk](solar-research-cover.png)

Image: illustrative composite; not a photograph of a verified site.

China's solar industry presents an unusual economic puzzle. The same expansion that has made photovoltaic equipment cheaper and more widely available has left some of its leading manufacturers deeply unprofitable. At the same time, an electricity system with an immense new fleet of solar installations has, in certain places and hours, been unable to use all the power they could produce. These observations are sometimes treated as evidence that the solar transition has gone too far. They are better understood as evidence that the transition has moved beyond its first bottleneck. The problem is no longer only whether inexpensive panels can be manufactured at scale. It is whether firms can earn a durable return on producing them, and whether a power system can deliver their output at the times and places it has value. Manufacturing capacity, installed capacity, and usable electricity are three distinct quantities. China's experience shows what happens when they expand at different speeds.

The scale of the achievement should not be minimized. The International Energy Agency's Photovoltaic Power Systems Programme (IEA PVPS) estimates that China installed between 309 and 357 gigawatts (GW) of photovoltaics in 2024, nearly 60 percent of global additions. The range reflects differences in accounting for new installations rather than uncertainty about whether the market was large. Globally, between 553 and 601 GW were added that year, and cumulative PV capacity exceeded 2,260 GW [1]. By the end of 2025, China's National Energy Administration (NEA) reported 1.2 billion kilowatts, or 1,200 GW, of installed photovoltaic capacity. It reported 317 GW of additions during 2025 and 1.17 trillion kilowatt-hours of solar electricity generated, 40 percent more than in 2024 [2]. Installed capacity is the potential rate of output under specified conditions; generation is energy actually produced over time. They should not be substituted for one another.

Yet the expansion of installations has not translated into prosperity for every producer of panels. In its *Renewables 2025* assessment, the International Energy Agency (IEA) reported that Chinese PV prices had fallen by more than 60 percent since 2023, a decline it associated with a glut of modules and intense competition for market share. The weighted average net margin of the large manufacturers in its analysis had dropped to roughly minus 10 percent, and their cumulative losses since the beginning of 2024 approached US$5 billion [3]. These are estimates for the IEA's analyzed companies, not a loss account for the entire Chinese solar sector. Reuters' examination of the first half of 2025 found that several prominent firms reported widening losses; Longi Green Energy was a notable exception, with a smaller loss than a year earlier [4]. The precise fortunes of individual producers differ. The broader pattern, however, is difficult to reconcile with a simple story in which rising sales must make every manufacturer better off.

The economic mechanism is less mysterious than the juxtaposition of records and losses suggests. A factory represents an investment made before its output is sold. When many firms expand simultaneously, each can rationally try to keep lines operating and spread fixed costs over more panels, even as aggregate production pushes prices lower. A producer may accept a price that contributes toward operating expenses in the short run without earning enough to replace its equipment or remunerate its capital over the long run. The decisions can be individually defensible and collectively destructive. This argument does not require an assertion that every firm sells below its variable cost, and public summary statistics do not establish such a claim. It does explain how an industry can improve the affordability of its product while weakening the balance sheets of its producers.

That distinction between the social benefit of cheap equipment and the private return on making it is central to the case. Chinese industrial policy helped build a highly capable supply chain: the IEA's 2022 examination credited policy support, economies of scale, and innovation with substantial cost reductions, while warning that concentrated production could expose buyers elsewhere to supply disruptions [5]. The report describes the historical formation of the industry; its market-share figures should not be passed off as a measurement of the 2025 or 2026 market. In the later *Renewables 2025* assessment, the IEA again saw both sides of the bargain: manufacturers' finances were strained, but renewable developers and buyers still wanted equipment [3]. A price war can transfer value to project builders and electricity customers even if it leaves too little value with panel manufacturers. Whether that transfer is sustainable depends partly on whether firms can continue to invest in quality and innovation while earning negative returns.

Cheaper panels, moreover, do not imply that solar electricity becomes cheaper by the same percentage. A power plant requires land, installation, financing, an interconnection, and operations; its economics also depend on how much electricity it generates and what that electricity is worth. The International Renewable Energy Agency (IRENA) found that, despite lower installed costs, the global weighted-average levelized cost of utility-scale solar electricity rose slightly, by 0.6 percent, between 2023 and 2024 as other factors changed [6]. Levelized cost distributes a project's lifetime expenditure across its expected lifetime output. It is not a wholesale electricity price, a household tariff, or a measure of what an additional unit of solar power is worth on a particular afternoon. IRENA also estimated that 91 percent of new utility-scale renewable capacity commissioned in 2024 could produce electricity at a lower levelized cost than the cheapest new fossil-fuel alternative [6]. Both findings can be true. A technology can be broadly competitive while a decline in one equipment price fails to reduce every project's all-in cost.

There is a second mismatch at the point where the panel becomes part of a power system. Photovoltaic generation follows daylight and weather. Demand follows factories, homes, temperature, and the hour of the day. Transmission links places of production to places of consumption, while storage and flexible consumption can shift the time at which energy is used. When the system cannot absorb available output, operators curtail generation: they deliberately reduce what a plant supplies to the grid. Curtailment is a measure of forgone *potential generation*, not a percentage of panels permanently out of service. It can result from congestion or from the need to keep supply and demand in balance. Some curtailment may be cheaper than building infrastructure to capture every last kilowatt-hour; persistent or rising curtailment is nonetheless evidence that the value of additional generation depends on complementary investments.

China's official results put both the progress and the remaining constraint in view. The NEA's annual release reported that 95 percent of available photovoltaic generation was used in 2025, alongside the 1.17 trillion kilowatt-hours actually generated [2]. Reuters, citing the National New Energy Consumption Monitoring and Early Warning Center, reported a solar curtailment rate of 6.6 percent for the first half of 2025, up from 3.9 percent in the first half of 2024; the national average concealed much higher rates in particular provinces [7]. The annual utilization figure and the half-year curtailment figure are not a contradiction to be corrected by subtracting one from the other. They describe different reporting periods and potentially different measurement conventions. Together they indicate that most available solar output was absorbed nationally, even as constraints worsened in parts of the system during part of the year. The evidence does not establish that the glut of manufactured panels *caused* curtailment: the former is a product-market imbalance, the latter an electricity-system condition.

Geography sharpens the problem. Large solar resources can be far from the strongest centers of consumption, and the distribution networks that connect smaller projects are not interchangeable with long-distance transmission lines. In a study of distributed energy resources in China, the IEA reported that 11 provinces faced congestion or connection restrictions in 2024 when local demand or distribution investment could not keep pace with rooftop and other distributed PV [8]. The NEA's 2025 capacity data show why both levels of the network matter: of roughly 317 GW of new PV, about 164 GW came from centralized projects and 153 GW from distributed projects [2]. Those are different connection problems. An additional high-voltage line does not automatically relieve a neighborhood transformer's limit, while strengthening a local feeder does not move distant solar output across provinces.

Money invested in generation is therefore not identical to money invested in deliverable electricity. The IEA reported more than US$625 billion of Chinese clean-energy investment in 2024 and projected US$88 billion in transmission and distribution investment in 2025 [9]. The magnitudes alone cannot tell us whether either category is at its efficient level, because they do not measure the cost or output of an individual project. They do show that the grid is already a major investment priority, not an afterthought. In its broader 2026 electricity assessment, the IEA described grid queues and congestion as a growing global impediment to connecting new generation, storage, and demand [10]. Global queue numbers should not be mistaken for Chinese estimates. Their relevance is analytical: panel factories can expand more quickly than networks whose planning, permits, and construction take years.

Physical capacity is only one part of the adjustment. Electricity prices and operating rules determine whether resources respond to scarcity or surplus. An IEA analysis of China's power-system flexibility found that more than 90 percent of traded electricity was bound by medium- to long-term contracts, limiting the scope for volumes and prices to adjust to conditions in particular hours. It identified better economic dispatch, functioning spot markets, and access for storage and demand response as important means of accommodating variable renewable generation [11]. This is not an argument to abandon contracts: they can reduce financial risk and support investment. The question is whether contracts leave sufficient room for the system to reward generation and consumption that can shift when the grid needs them.

Policy is beginning to acknowledge the change in the constraint. In a notice issued in February 2025, China's National Development and Reform Commission and National Energy Administration directed a broader move toward market-based pricing for renewable electricity [12]. A market price can reveal that power generated at one hour or location is worth less than power delivered at another. It cannot, by itself, construct a line, improve a local connection, or make a battery profitable under rules that prevent it from selling the services it provides. The logic of reform is complementary rather than sequential: network capacity, storage, flexible demand, and market access have to develop together. The IEA's distributed-energy study specifically recommended better information about local hosting capacity and more room for flexible loads and aggregators to participate in power markets [8].

These choices also involve distributional trade-offs. If producers close factories, some workers and communities bear concentrated costs even if buyers benefit from cheaper equipment. If generators are exposed to lower midday electricity prices, projects conceived under earlier revenue assumptions can become less attractive, even if more accurate prices improve the system's long-run efficiency. If transmission is expanded without regard to the location and timing of demand, it may be expensive infrastructure with limited use. Conversely, a narrow focus on protecting incumbent panel firms could keep equipment costly and delay projects that would otherwise provide valuable electricity. None of these outcomes follows mechanically from a single national curtailment rate or a single company's financial statement. A sound policy assessment must compare alternatives at the margin and be explicit about whose costs are counted.

It is tempting to answer the excess-capacity problem with a general demand for more installations. This would confuse demand for panels with demand for electricity at a useful place and time. It is equally tempting to demand fewer installations wherever curtailment exists. That would disregard the possibility that some curtailment is economically tolerable and that solar may displace more expensive or more polluting generation at other hours. The better test is local and temporal: how much additional electricity will a proposed project actually deliver, what other generation will it displace, and what complementary grid or flexibility investment does it require? Bruegel argued in 2025 that China could gain more from redirecting some emphasis toward power-grid investment than from continuing to expand renewable manufacturing indiscriminately [13]. That is a plausible interpretation of the constraint, not a measured nationwide cost-benefit result.

A practical assessment would follow three accounts rather than a single headline total. The first is the factory account: utilization of productive capacity, margins over the business cycle, inventories, and the ability to finance equipment upgrades. The second is the project account: installation cost, financing cost, actual generation, and expected revenue by hour. The third is the system account: power delivered after curtailment, avoided fuel and emissions, network cost, reliability, and the value of flexibility. Treating these accounts separately would prevent a fall in module prices from being mistaken for an equal fall in electricity costs, or an increase in installed gigawatts from being mistaken for a proportional increase in useful power. The most consequential policy question is how to connect the three, not which one can produce the largest number.

There are limits to what the present evidence can prove. The statistics assembled here come from different institutions, years, and populations: the IEA's manufacturer sample, official Chinese capacity and generation totals, a first-half curtailment series reported by Reuters, and international project-cost estimates. They are not inputs to a common regression and should not be combined into an invented estimate of the economic loss attributable to oversupply. The analysis establishes a coherent mechanism supported by observed conditions, not a causal decomposition of each company's losses or each province's forgone output. Testing the strongest policy claim would require plant-level prices and costs, hourly generation and dispatch, local network constraints, and credible comparisons of proposed grid and storage projects. Those data may change which intervention is best in a particular place.

The significance of the solar paradox lies in its sequence. China succeeded at the industrial problem of building a vast, low-cost supply of panels. The associated competition now tests the financial endurance of those who produce them. It succeeded at installing panels at a scale that would have seemed implausible a decade ago. Their output now tests the adaptability of the grid, market institutions, and patterns of demand. Nothing about manufacturer losses negates the value of affordable clean-energy equipment; nothing about record capacity guarantees that each new panel creates equal value. The next phase of the energy transition will be judged less by how many panels a country can make than by how much reliable, valuable electricity it can deliver from them.
